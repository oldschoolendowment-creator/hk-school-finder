Hong Kong schools, for parents used to England

What works differently, and what catches English families out. Rules are for entry in September 2027, from the Education Bureau (EDB). Always check the EDB website before you act.

1. "Catchment" means a school net, and closeness doesn't count

Hong Kong is split into about 35 Primary One school nets. Hong Kong Island has five: 11 (Central and Western), 12 (Wan Chai and Happy Valley), 14 (North Point and Quarry Bay), 16 (Shau Kei Wan to Chai Wan) and 18 (Southern District). The map shows them as coloured outlines.

Unlike an English "last distance offered", a school net has a hard edge. Inside it, every address counts the same: a flat across the road from a school has no better chance than one at the far end of the net. Outside it, your chances at that school are small.

That is why some nets on the Island, such as nets 11 and 12, are especially sought after, and why flats there can cost more.

Watch out: the net goes by your child's actual home address, backed by proof such as a stamped tenancy agreement or utility bills. Using a false address is a criminal offence (a false document can carry up to 14 years in prison), and any place you got will be withdrawn.

2. How a Primary One place is decided

This only covers government and aided primaries. DSS, private, ESF and international schools all admit separately (see section 5).

  1. Discretionary Places (about half of each school's places). You apply in September of the year before entry, to one government or aided school only, in any net. Applying to two cancels both applications.
    • Children with a brother or sister at the school, or a parent working there, get in automatically (about 30% of places).
    • At least 20% of places go by points. Choose one of these: a parent works full-time in a kindergarten or secondary on the same site, or a sibling attends the secondary on the same site (20); a parent is a school manager (20); a parent or sibling is a former pupil (10); first-born child (5). Then add one of: same religion as the school's sponsor, or a parent is a member of the sponsoring organisation (5). Finally, 10 points if the child is the right age (5 years 8 months to 7).
    • Your address earns no points. Ties are broken by drawing lots. Schools are not allowed to test or interview children.
    • Results: 23 November 2026. You must register at the school on 25 or 26 November or lose the place.
  2. Central Allocation (the other half). If you didn't get a discretionary place, you take part automatically. In January 2027 you list:
    • Up to 3 schools from any net (10% of the Central Allocation places).
    • Your ranked choices from inside your own net (the other 90%).
    Oversubscribed schools are filled by random number. Results arrive 2 to 3 June 2027.

Applications go through the ePOA website (epoa.edb.gov.hk) or on paper. In the app, put your address in the Menu to see your net and the primaries you can choose from.

3. Your primary school shapes the secondary allocation

Secondary places (SSPA) also use nets. On Hong Kong Island each secondary net is a whole district: HK1 Central and Western, HK2 Wan Chai, HK3 Eastern, HK4 Southern.

  • Up to 30% of places are given at a Discretionary Places stage in Primary 6. You can apply to two schools anywhere, and schools may interview.
  • Everyone else is put into one of three allocation bands. Bands come from the child's school marks in P5 and P6, scaled by how well their primary school does in a government test. Band 1 children are placed first.
  • This means the primary your child attends affects their band, so families think about secondary when choosing primary.
  • Some primaries are linked to a secondary as through-train, feeder or nominated schools, and pupils get some or all of their places there. A feeder link lets the secondary keep up to 85% of its first-year places for that primary's pupils, and a nominated link up to 25%. The app shows all three kinds of link in each primary's panel.
  • "Band 1 school" means a secondary that mostly takes Band 1 pupils. It is the nearest Hong Kong has to a league table, but it is not official.

4. Language

  • Most government and aided primaries teach mainly in Cantonese, with English and often Mandarin as subjects. Children learn traditional Chinese characters.
  • Secondaries teach mainly in either English (EMI) or Chinese (CMI). Many CMI schools teach some subjects in English.
  • For a child with no Chinese, a local school is a big step. Some schools have extra support for non-Chinese-speaking (NCS) children; the EDB funds this, and kindergarten profiles in the app say whether a kindergarten gets that grant.
  • This is why many English-speaking families look at ESF, international or English-leaning DSS schools instead.

6. Kindergarten is a competition of its own

  • There is no state nursery and no allocation. You apply direct to as many kindergartens as you like.
  • Children start K1 in the September after they reach 2 years 8 months. Popular kindergartens take applications around September to November the year before, and many hold short interviews or play sessions for toddlers.
  • To accept a place at a scheme kindergarten you hand over a K1 Registration Certificate, which you get from the EDB. You can only hold one at a time.
  • Scheme kindergartens: half-day places are free or nearly free; whole-day places cost extra. Non-scheme kindergartens (often international) set their own fees.
  • Many kindergartens still run separate morning and afternoon sessions. The app lets you filter by session.

7. Other things that surprise English families

  • No published quality data. The EDB visits schools and publishes kindergarten "Quality Review" results (shown in the app), but there are no Ofsted-style grades for primaries or secondaries and no official exam tables. School profiles on chsc.hk are the best official source: class sizes, homework policy, teachers' qualifications, linked secondaries.
  • Faith is everywhere. Most aided schools have a church or religious sponsor. Unlike many English faith schools, religion gives only 5 points, and only at the Discretionary Places stage.
  • Siblings are guaranteed a place at the Discretionary Places stage, which is stronger than most English sibling rules.
  • Moving house matters. Plan where you live before the January Central Allocation, as your net is based on where you actually live.
  • Residency. Your child must be allowed to live in Hong Kong (for example on a dependant visa) to join the government systems.
  • Costs at "free" schools. Parents usually buy textbooks, workbooks and uniforms themselves.
  • Weather closures. Schools close for Typhoon Signal 8 and Black Rainstorm warnings; the EDB announces closures.
  • Tutoring and homework are a much bigger part of school life than in most English primaries.
